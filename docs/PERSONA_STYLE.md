# The Persona sound: what it's made of, and what MouthMixer takes from it

A breakdown of the Persona 3 / 4 / 5 soundtracks (Shoji Meguro, with Atsushi Kitajoh on P3 Reload;
vocalists Yumi Kawamura, Lotus Juice, Shihoko Hirata, Lyn Inaizumi, Azumi Takahashi), turned into style
rules. LINE ZERO's house rule (AUDIO_PLAN §3.1) applies: take the **genre traits**, never the
**fingerprints**.

**How this was researched (2026-10-12):** web search over reviews and analyses (RPGFan, Square Enix Music,
VGMO, The Ongaku, Siliconera's report of a 2015 Famitsu interview, Hooktheory, Wikipedia, interviews with
Lotus Juice). Page fetches were blocked, so sourced claims rest on search excerpts; **[U]** marks genre
knowledge not backed by a source. Hooktheory/Musicnotes BPMs are crowd-sourced and approximate.

---

## 1. One genre identity per game

- **P3 (2006):** hip-hop and techno with J-pop vocals and rap; critics tie it to the urban setting. Lotus
  Juice: Meguro "didn't want the music to interfere with the players… just wanted something like a rap
  feel." https://www.rpgfan.com/music-review/persona-3-original-soundtrack/ ·
  https://www.animeherald.com/2024/05/23/mass-destruction-a-conversation-with-lotus-juice/
  - P3 Reload (2024): "cleaner, better-balanced instrumentals"; some fans feel it lost punch, a lesson that
    **density and punch are part of the identity**. https://www.rpgfan.com/music-review/persona-3-reload-original-soundtrack/
- **P4 (2008):** J-pop and pop-rock with funk, "funky piano", "distorted trumpets", surf guitar on the
  battle theme; vocals even on daily-life and dungeon loops; upbeat "retro pop".
  https://squareenixmusic.com/reviews/furyoffrog/persona4sideb.shtml · https://www.theongaku.com/posts/reach-out-to-the-truth-the-story-of-persona-4-vocal-tracks
- **P5 (2016):** acid jazz, funk, big band and lounge, with rock/metal moments. Meguro (Famitsu, 2015):
  he "incorporated acid jazz-like elements to bring out the game's mood".
  https://www.siliconera.com/persona-5s-soundtrack-comes-acid-jazz-influences/
- **Meguro's roots:** a rock guitarist; influences T-Square, Casiopea (Japanese fusion) and Herb Alpert
  (brass pop). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoji_Meguro
- "City pop" is a fan/market label; no source calls a Persona score city pop.

**Lesson for MouthMixer:** each *game* (or each LINE ZERO area) picks one genre identity and keeps it across
field, battle and menus. That's why packs are whole palettes, not single instruments.

## 2. Tempo, key, meter

| Cue type | Examples | BPM | Key |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal battle (P5) | Last Surprise | ~123 | D minor |
| Action / infiltration | Life Will Change; P5 opening | ~126-132 | minor |
| Final boss | Ark | ~108 | B minor |
| Rock battle (P4) | Reach Out to the Truth | ~170 (arrangement) | B minor in practice |
| Night / reflective field | Beneath the Mask; Aria of the Soul | ~75-92 | G minor; B minor |
| Jazz club | No More What Ifs | ~140, swing | D major |

Sources: Hooktheory theorytabs for each title; Musicnotes. Everything is 4/4; minor keys dominate the action
cues (D and G minor recur). Swing appears only in jazz-club pieces; the funk grooves are straight 16ths. [U]

## 3. Harmony

- "Last Surprise": chord complexity 91/100 (7ths, add9s, borrowed notes), melody 96% diatonic but **often on
  extensions over rich chords** (chord-melody tension 69); VI is its most-used chord.
- "Life Will Change": complexity 29, i chord dominant: **big anthem choruses use simple minor loops**.
- "Beneath the Mask": iv(sus4) most used; "Rivers in the Desert": bVII; "Ark": III; "Colors Flying High":
  diminished 7ths and borrowed chords; "Mementos": Locrian for dungeon unease.
  (all https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/shoji-meguro/...)
- [U] general acid-jazz practice: m7/m9 on i and iv, maj7 on bVI and bIII, 7#9 funk stabs, ii-V into
  choruses, chromatic planing of 7th chords, bVI-bVII-i cadences.

**Encoded:** rich verses and simple choruses: the "heist funk" pack harmonises A sections with sevenths and
ninths and its favourite loops are bVI-bVII-i and i-iv-bVII-bIII.

## 4. Rhythm section and keys

- Bass: "organs and slap bass are everywhere"; "funky basslines" and "pulsing drums" (P5).
  https://www.rpgfan.com/music-review/persona-5-original-soundtrack/ — [U] octave-jumping 16ths with ghost
  notes, pushes on the "and" of 4.
- Drums: P3 programmed hip-hop/techno ("bouncy hip-hop"); P5 a "live-band feel". [U] breakbeats with busy
  open hats.
- Keys: organ (P5), funky piano (P4), electric piano on softer tracks.
- Guitar: rock is the trademark; "surfing guitar lines" on P4's battle theme. [U] wah and 16th cutting
  guitar on P5's funk tracks.

## 5. Brass and winds

- "Big-band style trumpets blasting" (P3); "distorted trumpets" (P4); big band in P5 (a P5 big-band concert;
  a Grammy-nominated big-band arrangement of "Last Surprise").
  https://vgmonline.net/persona3-2/ · https://megatenwiki.com/wiki/Persona_5_Special_Big_Band_Concert_2025
- [U] brass works as short stabs on anticipations and hooks between vocal lines, not long solos.

**Encoded:** a "brass hook" riff part and brass stabs on anticipations; **trumpet** carries the brass identity,
not saxophone (sax as the battle lead is a fingerprint).

## 6. Catchiness: why it's addictive

- **Short, chantable hooks** spread the songs ("You'll never see it comin'", "Look into my eyes").
- **Riffs repeat every 2 bars** so the ear locks on. [U]
- **No long intros** in battle tracks; they're heard hundreds of times. [U]
- **Density and punch** matter (the P3 Reload "lost punch" complaints).
- **Separate cues per game state** (normal / ambush / boss), sharing a palette.

**Encoded:** every Persona-informed pack writes a 2-bar **keyboard or brass riff** (the hook) from A' on;
intros are 2-4 bars; parts build so the last A'' is the densest.

## 7. Vocals

- Singers: Lyn Inaizumi (P5) — soul/R&B, Donny Hathaway-inspired, wide range, fluent English; Shihoko Hirata
  (P4) — bright J-pop; Yumi Kawamura + Lotus Juice (P3) — sung hooks plus rap verses; Azumi Takahashi
  (P3R) — her voice cut up "almost like DJ scratching" on one battle track.
  https://automaton-media.com/en/news/20231222-24761/ · https://animecons.com/guests/bio/4830/lotus-juice
- Lyrics are English, often by non-native writers ("Engrish" is a common review word); short repeated
  hooks; P4 puts vocals even on field loops.
- [U] call and response between singer and rapper; doubled choruses; light effects (reverb, delay) apart
  from the P3R chopped vocal.

**Encoded:** the vocal presets "soul" (warm, natural tuning, plate) and "bright pop" (firmer tuning, doubles
on the hook) in `docs/VOCALS.md`; the lyric writer is asked for short, chantable hooks repeated 2-4 times.

## 8. Fingerprints we never copy vs. traits we use

**Never:** the hook phrases and melodies above; the exact riffs of "Last Surprise" or "Wake Up, Get Up, Get
Out There"; the Velvet Room soprano-and-organ aria; imitating a specific singer's voice; the chopped
"DJ-scratch" vocal on a battle cue; the whole package of soul female singer + English rap verse in a minor
key at ~123-132 BPM with slap bass and organ as the main battle theme; saxophone as the battle lead.

**Use freely (genre vocabulary):** acid jazz, funk, J-pop, hip-hop and rock; m7/m9/sus chords, bVI-bVII-i,
diminished passing chords, modal dungeon colour; 120-135 BPM action and 75-95 BPM daily-life cues; slap or
finger bass, breakbeats, Rhodes and organ comping, brass stabs, wah guitar; English vocal hooks; separate
cues per game state; simple chant choruses over richer verse harmony.

## 9. Gaps

No verified BPM/key for "Mass Destruction", "It's Going Down Now" or "Rivers in the Desert"; the "Beneath the

## 10. Making yours sound more like Persona (for the person humming)

What you do in the booth matters more than any setting. These follow from §2-§7; none of them copy a song.

**The hook**
- Keep it short: 2 bars, sung 2-4 times. If you can't chant it after hearing it twice, it's too long.
- Start it off the beat (on the "and" just before beat 1) and give it one jump of a 4th or more: pushed
  rhythms and a leap make funk and J-pop hooks stick.
- In the battle and night starters, land phrase ends on the 9th or 7th of the chord instead of the root:
  hum a step above or below where it "wants" to end. That jazzy, unresolved landing is the acid-jazz
  sound (§3: melody on extensions over rich chords).
- Leave gaps. The space between hook phrases is where the answer line or a brass stab goes (call and
  response, §7).

**Rhythm**
- Battle starters: think in 16ths. Bassline: short notes, octave jumps ("dum, dum-DUM"), a push on the
  "and" of 4 into the next bar (§4). Beatbox: busy hats, ghost snares (soft "k" between the loud ones).
- Night starters: lay back. Sing a hair behind the beat; the timing correction keeps your average lateness
  as feel.
- Accent stabs: put them on the "and" of 2 and the "and" of 4, or just before bar 1. Short and loud.

**Sounds to pick for your pieces**
- Hook: trumpet or muted trumpet (brass identity, §5), Rhodes, organ, clean or chorus guitar. Avoid
  saxophone as the battle lead (it's a fingerprint, §8).
- Riff under everything: Rhodes, clavinet or organ, 2 bars, repeating.
- Bass: slap bass for battle, finger or synth bass at night.
- Stabs: brass section. Vocal chops: one or two syllables ("hey", "ah", "go").

**Structure**
- No long intros in battle tracks: 4 bars at most, then the groove (§6).
- Make the last hook the densest: switch on extra percussion, the stabs and the chop only in the final
  hook sections on the song board.
- Bridge: drop the beat to half (switch the beat off and keep percussion), then bring everything back.
- Make one track per game state with the same pieces: a calm version (no drums, pad and riff), the
  normal version, and a boss version (boss starter, same hook). That's how one identity runs through a
  game (§1, §6).

**Vocals**
- Short English hook lines, sung, with simpler verses. Doubles on the hook (bright-pop preset) for the P4
  sound; the soul preset with natural tuning for the P5 sound.
- Don't imitate a specific singer's voice (§8); sing it your way.
